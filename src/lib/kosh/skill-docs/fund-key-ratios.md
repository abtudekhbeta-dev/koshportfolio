# Key Ratios & Metrics — Intelligent Multi-Bagger Analysis

Purpose: identify companies capable of compounding earnings and intrinsic value over 3–7 years. Historical metrics provide context; future economics matter more.

## 1. Core Interpretation Rule

Never interpret a major metric in isolation.

Use this sequence where relevant:

Metric → Change → Drivers → Driver Quality → Related-Metric Cross-check → Contradictions → Investment Implication

A metric is an observation, not a conclusion.

Do not use universal rules such as:

* High ROE = automatically good
* Low P/E = automatically cheap
* High promoter ownership = automatically positive
* High growth = automatically high quality
* High debt/equity = automatically dangerous

Interpret metrics according to the company's business model, sector economics, capital intensity, cyclicality, competitive position, accounting structure and reinvestment needs.

If the underlying driver cannot be established reliably, retain the conventional metric analysis and state the limitation. Never invent causation.

## 2. Growth

### Historical

Assess where available:

* Revenue CAGR — 3Y and 5Y
* PAT/EPS CAGR — 3Y and 5Y
* EBITDA growth
* Margin trajectory

### Forward

Assess:

* Revenue growth
* EPS/PAT growth
* EBITDA growth
* Margin trajectory
* Organic versus acquisition-driven growth

Do not judge growth by percentage alone.

Where relevant investigate:

* Volume
* Price
* Product/service mix
* Market share
* Capacity
* Utilization
* New products
* Geography
* Acquisitions
* Operating leverage
* Addressable market

Then cross-check growth against:

Profit + Margins + Receivables + Inventory + CFO + Working Capital + ROCE

The key question:

Can high growth persist for years while earning attractive returns on the capital required to support it?

## 3. Profitability & Margins

Interpret profit growth together with revenue growth.

Assess whether profit is growing:

* Faster than revenue
* In line with revenue
* Slower than revenue
* Despite declining revenue

Where relevant determine whether margin changes are driven by:

* Pricing
* Mix
* Input costs
* Operating leverage
* Efficiency
* Utilization
* Temporary/cyclical factors
* One-off cost reductions

Distinguish structural improvement from temporary improvement.

Cross-check PAT against EBITDA/EBIT, CFO and exceptional/other-income effects.

## 4. ROE

Where data permits, conceptually assess:

ROE ≈ Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

Determine whether high/improving ROE is primarily driven by:

* Better profitability
* Better asset efficiency
* Greater leverage
* Capital-base changes

Cross-check:

ROE + ROCE + Debt + Margins

Examples:

ROE ↑ + ROCE ↑ + Debt stable/falling = stronger evidence of genuine operational improvement.

ROE ↑ + ROCE flat/down + Debt ↑ = weaker quality of ROE improvement.

## 5. ROCE / ROIC

Assess:

* Absolute level
* Trend
* Capital intensity
* Asset efficiency
* Working-capital requirements
* Utilization
* Leverage
* Invested-capital changes

Do not rely only on historical ROCE.

Where possible assess:

Incremental Operating Profit / Incremental Invested Capital

for:

* New factories
* Stores/branches
* New capacity
* Products
* Acquisitions
* Geographic expansion

Distinguish:

Returns on the existing business

from

Returns on future growth capital.

High historical ROCE with poor incremental returns can indicate weakening future economics.

## 6. Reinvestment Economics

A potential compounding machine generally requires:

Large runway × High reinvestment opportunity × High incremental returns

Assess:

* Reinvestment rate
* Capex requirements
* Working-capital requirements
* Acquisition requirements
* Expected return on incremental capital

A company cannot compound rapidly for long if it lacks productive opportunities to reinvest.

## 7. Cash Flow & Earnings Quality

Cross-check:

PAT ↔ CFO ↔ FCF

Assess:

* OCF/PAT
* FCF/PAT
* FCF margin
* Cash-flow trend
* Working-capital movements

Do not mechanically treat PAT/CFO divergence as negative.

Investigate whether divergence is caused by:

* Receivables
* Inventory
* Payables
* Growth investment
* Timing
* Non-cash items
* Temporary working-capital release

Persistent unexplained divergence deserves a less favorable assessment.

## 8. Working Capital

Track:

* Receivable days
* Inventory days
* Payable days
* Cash conversion cycle

Compare changes against revenue growth.

Particularly important:

Revenue growth vs Receivables growth

Revenue growth vs Inventory growth

Rapidly rising receivables/inventory alongside weak cash conversion can reduce growth-quality confidence.

Context matters; do not mechanically classify every increase as negative.

## 9. Balance Sheet & Leverage

Assess:

* Net Debt/EBITDA
* Debt/Equity
* Interest coverage
* Net cash
* Debt maturity
* Refinancing needs
* Foreign-currency exposure where relevant

Never judge debt using a universal threshold.

Ask:

What is the company getting in return for additional leverage?

Cross-check:

Debt + EBITDA + EBIT + Interest + CFO + ROCE + Growth

Debt can be productive when it funds high-return expansion; the same debt can be dangerous when returns and cash generation deteriorate.

## 10. EPS & Share Count

Compare:

PAT growth vs EPS growth vs Share Count

Assess:

* Dilution
* Buybacks
* ESOPs
* Warrants
* Convertibles

Distinguish:

Underlying business earnings growth

from

Per-share earnings growth caused partly by share-count changes.

## 11. Valuation

Assess where meaningful:

P/E

* Trailing
* Forward
* Historical range
* Peer comparison

EV/EBITDA

* Trailing
* Forward

P/B

Especially relevant for financials and asset-heavy businesses.

PEG

Use as a screening aid, not a standalone conclusion.

Shareholder Returns

Dividend yield and buyback yield where meaningful.

Never conclude:

Low multiple = cheap

or

High multiple = expensive

without considering:

* Growth
* Duration
* ROCE
* Margins
* Cash quality
* Balance sheet
* Cyclicality
* Competitive durability

## 12. Valuation Reverse Engineering

Always ask:

What future performance is today's price already pricing in?

Where possible estimate:

* Revenue growth required
* EPS growth required
* Margin assumptions
* Reinvestment requirements
* Return on incremental capital
* Terminal economics
* Multiple assumptions

For cyclical companies, use normalized economics rather than blindly using peak/trough earnings.

The key question:

How much future success is already reflected in today's price?

## 13. Cross-Metric Intelligence

Actively connect economically related metrics.

Growth Quality

Revenue + Profit + Margin + Receivables + CFO

Return Quality

ROE + ROCE + Debt + Margin + Asset Efficiency

EPS Quality

PAT + EPS + Share Count

Leverage Quality

Debt + EBITDA + Interest + CFO + ROCE

Reinvestment Quality

Capex + Revenue + Incremental Returns + ROCE

Valuation Quality

P/E + Growth + ROCE + Margin Sustainability + Cyclicality

Ownership Quality

Promoter Holding + Pledge + Insider Activity + Dilution

Do not let one attractive ratio dominate the conclusion.

## 14. Contradiction Detection

Actively search for situations where headline metrics conflict with supporting evidence.

Examples:

High ROE + High leverage + Mediocre ROCE

Strong profit growth + Weak CFO + Rising receivables

Low P/E + Peak-cycle margins

Strong FCF + Temporary working-capital release

Strong EPS growth + Slower PAT growth + Falling share count

Large order book + Weak revenue/cash conversion

Large capacity expansion + Weak utilization

Contradictions should be surfaced when material.

Do not automatically classify every contradiction as negative; determine the reason and persistence.

## 15. Positive Evidence Convergence

Also identify reinforcing evidence.

Example:

Revenue ↑ + Profit ↑ faster + Margins ↑ + ROCE ↑ + Debt ↓ + CFO ↑

This is stronger evidence than six isolated positive ratios.

Likewise, multiple simultaneous deteriorations should increase concern.

## 16. Accounting & Presentation Quality

Where material, cross-check:

* Revenue recognition
* Receivables
* Inventory
* Contract assets/liabilities
* Capitalized costs
* Depreciation
* Goodwill/intangibles
* Other income
* Exceptional items
* Tax effects
* Related parties
* Subsidiaries/associates
* Dilution
* Buybacks
* Asset sales
* Accounting-policy changes

Treat management commentary as a claim to be tested against actual outcomes.

Do not accuse fraud or manipulation without evidence.

Instead identify:

* Weak corroboration
* Presentation risk
* Accounting-quality concern
* Economic mismatch

## 17. Sector-Aware Metrics

First identify how the company makes money and what economically drives value creation.

Then emphasize relevant metrics.

Banks

ROA, ROE, NIM, credit/deposit growth, GNPA/NNPA, slippages, provisions, credit cost, CASA, capital adequacy, funding.

NBFCs

AUM growth/quality, NIM/spreads, funding cost, leverage, liquidity, asset quality, credit cost.

Insurance

Premium growth, VNB, VNB margin, persistency, solvency, underwriting economics.

IT/Services

Organic/constant-currency growth, client concentration, productivity, utilization, EBIT margin, pricing, deal conversion, cash conversion.

Manufacturing/Industrial

Volume, realization, utilization, unit economics, input costs, capex productivity, working capital, ROCE, leverage.

Consumer

Volume vs price/mix, distribution/market expansion, margins, brand economics, ROCE.

Pharma

Product/geography mix, R&D, regulatory exposure, concentration, margins, working capital, cash.

These are guides, not rigid templates. Use the metrics that best test the actual company's economics.

## 18. Financial Sector Rule

Do not mechanically apply industrial-company metrics to banks, NBFCs, insurers or other financial businesses.

Use sector-appropriate measures and interpret them together.

## 19. Forward Metrics

When reliably available, obtain:

* Management revenue guidance
* EBITDA guidance
* PAT/EPS guidance
* Volume/capacity guidance
* Capex guidance
* Street revenue estimate
* Street EPS/PAT estimate
* Expected EPS growth
* Forward P/E
* Forward EV/EBITDA where meaningful
* PEG where meaningful
* Expected margin trajectory
* Valuation-implied expectations

Always provide source + date.

If unavailable:

Not reliably available

Never fabricate.

## 20. Core Investment Test

Every important metric should ultimately help answer:

1. Can earnings grow rapidly?
2. Can growth persist for years?
3. Can new capital earn attractive returns?
4. Is management credible?
5. Is governance sufficiently clean?
6. What future success is already priced in?
7. Is upside meaningfully larger than downside?

The final assessment should represent the combined economics, not a mechanical average of individual ratios.
